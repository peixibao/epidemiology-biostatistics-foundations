## Block 1.1A — From a Research Topic to a Research Question
**Status:** **Formally taught 2026-10-21; learner confirmed understanding 2026-10-28.**

### Scope and learning objective
Understand how a broad **research topic** becomes a focused **research question**, identify the relevant components when applicable, and distinguish a topic, question, objective and possible hypothesis. Full research-aim taxonomy, PICO/PECO and detailed methods remain in subsequent blocks.

### Definitions
- **Research topic（研究主题）:** a broad area of scientific interest; it identifies a subject but can contain many possible specific questions.
- **Research question（研究问题）:** a focused, answerable question specifying what scientific knowledge a study seeks to obtain or establish.
- **Research objective（研究目标）:** a statement of the task a study intends to accomplish, often expressed with an infinitive such as *to estimate*, *to examine* or *to compare*.
- **Research hypothesis（研究假设）:** a proposed answer or expected relationship, potentially directional, that can be examined against evidence. Some descriptive or exploratory studies do not require a formal directional hypothesis.

### General illustration
**Topic:** Air pollution and respiratory health.

**Research question:** *Among children aged 6–12 living in City A, is long-term exposure to PM2.5 associated with asthma exacerbations?*

| Component | What it specifies | Illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Population（研究总体）** | Who or what we seek to understand | Children aged 6–12 in City A |
| **Exposure（暴露）** | Factor or characteristic of interest | Long-term PM2.5 exposure |
| **Outcome（结局）** | Health-related endpoint | Asthma exacerbations |
| **Research aim（研究目的）** | Knowledge sought | Investigate an association |

An exposure in epidemiology is **not limited to toxic agents**: behavior, treatment, environmental conditions and other attributes may be considered exposures depending on the question. Identifying an exposure does not itself imply causation.

The four illustrated components are **context-dependent, not a mandatory template**. For example, *What was the prevalence of anemia among pregnant patients attending public antenatal clinics in City B during 2025?* is a descriptive frequency question with a population, health outcome and timeframe, but no specified exposure.

### Why the distinction matters
One broad topic can yield questions that demand different kinds of evidence. Estimating asthma frequency requires a defined population and valid disease ascertainment; examining PM2.5–exacerbation association requires meaningful exposure and outcome information and an analysis appropriate to the relationship. Hence the question helps specify **data needs, the quantity of interest and plausible research designs**. Ethical/practical constraints, available data and analytical assumptions still influence design selection.

### Topic vs question vs objective vs hypothesis
- Topic: *Air pollution and respiratory health*.
- Question: *Is long-term PM2.5 exposure associated with asthma exacerbations in children?*
- Objective: *To examine the association between long-term PM2.5 exposure and asthma exacerbations among children*.
- Optional hypothesis: *Higher long-term PM2.5 exposure is associated with more asthma exacerbations*.

## Block 1.1B — Four Common Research Aims
**Status:** **Formally taught 2026-10-28; awaiting learner review.**

### Learning objective and taxonomy scope
Recognize what kind of scientific knowledge an epidemiological question seeks and distinguish **descriptive, associational, causal, and predictive aims**. These are **four common pedagogical categories**, not an exhaustive or mutually exclusive taxonomy. Their classification concerns scientific purposes, not specific study designs or statistical tests.

**Common research topic:** Physical activity and cardiovascular health.

| Research aim | Primary question | Example | Core task |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Descriptive**（描述性） | What is happening and how common is it? | What proportion of adults meet physical activity recommendations? | Estimate or characterize frequency, distribution, attributes in a defined group. |
| **Associational**（关联性） | How are variables related? | Is physical activity associated with blood pressure? | Quantify observed statistical relationships, with attention to definition and confounding. |
| **Causal**（因果性） | What would change under an intervention/exposure contrast? | Would assigning structured exercise lower blood pressure compared with usual care? | Estimate effects of well-defined alternative conditions under suitable designs and assumptions. |
| **Predictive**（预测性） | What unknown or future outcome can we predict? | Can physical activity, age and BMI predict hypertension within five years? | Produce and evaluate predictions, especially on new data. |

### Explanations and statistical implications
- **Descriptive:** The quantity of interest might be prevalence or a proportion. Proper population definition, selection, denominator, and measurement are necessary for credible population descriptions.
- **Associational:** A statistical relationship (e.g. lower blood pressure among more active participants) does not automatically identify the effect of *changing* activity. Age and other factors may influence both.
- **Causal:** The aim is a comparison of outcomes under different intervention/exposure conditions, often described with **potential outcomes（潜在结局）**. The same participant generally cannot simultaneously be observed under both alternatives in an identical situation. Both randomized and observational designs can inform causal effects, but the assumptions and resulting strength of claims differ.
- **Predictive:** Focus on predictive performance and validity beyond development data, including whether discrimination and predicted probabilities are useful. Strong predictors need not be causes or suitable intervention targets.

### Discriminating example: a lighter and lung cancer
- Associational: Are lighter carriers more likely to have lung cancer?
- Predictive: Does lighter ownership add information for predicting lung cancer risk?
- Causal: Would giving a nonsmoker a lighter, without changing smoking behavior, alter their cancer risk?
A variable associated with smoking can be statistically associated with and potentially predict lung cancer without necessarily having a causal effect when intervened on.

### Distinctions and limits
1. **Research aim ≠ study design ≠ statistical technique.** Regression can serve association analysis, causal estimation under stronger assumptions, and prediction. A cross-sectional or cohort study can contain several aims.
2. **Association ≠ causation**, even after adjustment; **predictive usefulness ≠ causal actionability**.
3. The four aims can overlap within the same paper. For example, descriptive summaries and a prediction model can coexist.
4. This block introduces the purposes conceptually. Formal estimands, performance metrics, identification assumptions, and selection of designs/models are reserved for their mapped later lessons.

## Block 1.1C — Formulating an Answerable Research Question
**Status:** **Formally taught 2026-10-30 — pending learner review.**

### Objective
Translate a broad scientific question into a question precise enough to guide selection of participants, measurement, comparison, data collection, and initial analytic planning. Evaluate whether a meaningful question can feasibly and ethically be addressed.

### 1. Answerability requires defined components
**Vague question:** *Does poor sleep increase the risk of disease?*

The question leaves the **population**, the meaning of **poor sleep**, the specific **outcome**, and **outcome period** unresolved.

**More focused example:** *Among adults aged 30–60 without diabetes at baseline, is habitual sleep duration below 6 hours (versus 7–8 hours) associated with the 5-year incidence of type 2 diabetes?*

This defines important question-level components, although an operational protocol must still specify sleep measurement, case ascertainment, and covariate strategy.

### 2. PICO, PECO, and the optional time element
| Element | PICO | PECO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | Population or participants | Population or participants |
| I / E | Intervention | Exposure |
| C | Comparator or comparison | Comparator or comparison |
| O | Outcome | Outcome |
| T (optional) | Time horizon in PICOT | Time horizon in PECOT |

**PICO question:** Among adults with knee osteoarthritis, does a **12-week supervised strengthening program**, versus **an educational booklet**, improve knee pain **at 12 weeks**? Intervention dose, comparator details, and pain scale still need protocol definitions.

**PECO question:** Among community-dwelling adults aged 65+, is **baseline slow gait speed**, versus faster speed, associated with **≥1 fall** over the next **12 months**?

PICO/PECO are flexible guides. They do not establish randomization, study design, causal identification, or whether a question is scientifically valuable. Some descriptive, diagnostic, qualitative or prediction questions need different organization.

### 3. Operationalization (操作化定义)
The gait-speed question requires explicit observable definitions:

| Construct | Example protocol definition |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Community-dwelling people aged ≥65 at enrollment |
| Gait speed | Usual-pace walking speed in m/s on a standardized course |
| Slow exposure category | Speed <0.8 m/s at baseline |
| Comparator | Speed ≥0.8 m/s |
| Fall | Person unintentionally comes to rest on ground/floor/lower level |
| Observation window | 12 months after baseline |
| Outcome | Experiencing at least one fall during that window, identified through regular participant reports |

The 0.8-m/s cutoff is an illustrative, prespecified threshold that must be justified. Alternative ascertainment or definitions affect the interpretation. **Any fall** differs from **fall-related hospital admission**: the latter only captures a subset of events.

**Operationalization** turns abstract attributes into measurable indicators, classification rules, and recording processes. A polished sentence is not enough to establish valid measurement.

### 4. FINER — planning heuristic
| Criterion | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Feasible** | Can participants/data, measurement, time and resources support an answer? |
| **Interesting** | Would the answer matter to an intended audience? |
| **Novel** | Does it fill a meaningful gap, including valuable replication/generalization? |
| **Ethical** | Can the question be investigated with acceptable risks and safeguards? |
| **Relevant** | Could the findings inform science, clinical work or public health? |

**FINER is a qualitative guide, not a validated score.** If follow-up falls were never collected, a dataset with baseline gait speed alone cannot answer a **12-month fall-risk** question. Options include collecting follow-up, using suitable existing data, or revising the research question to match what the data support.

### 5. Three protocol-level pitfalls
1. *Is exercise associated with depression?* — intensity, frequency, duration or a validated activity score must be selected and justified.
2. *Does obesity predict cardiovascular disease?* — specify whether outcome is prevalent disease, incident disease over a defined horizon, or mortality; those require different data and interpretation.
3. *Does air pollution cause asthma?* — causal wording requires a defensible intervention/exposure contrast, temporality, handling of confounding/selection and credible identification assumptions; statistical significance alone does not address the causal question.

### Self-check application
*Is social media use associated with anxiety among university students?* Define social media construct and measure, anxiety symptoms vs diagnosis, temporal structure, target population/recruitment, and whether the goal is association, prediction or an intervention effect.

## Block 1.1D — From Research Questions to Estimands, Evidence, and Study Design
**Status:** **Formally taught 2026-10-30 — learner review pending.**

### Learning objective
Define the target quantity that answers a question, distinguish **estimand**, **estimator** and **estimate**, and link that target to needed data and feasible study-design choices. The purpose is a conceptual bridge; technical assumptions and inference are developed in later chapters.

### 1. Why a research question alone is insufficient
Consider the earlier question: *Among community-dwelling adults aged 65 years or older, is slow gait speed associated with experiencing at least one fall during the following 12 months?*

Its answer depends on the quantity selected: an **absolute 12-month risk difference**, a **risk ratio**, another association measure, or a different outcome summary. Those describe different aspects of the data.

An **estimand（目标估计量）** is the *precisely defined population-level quantity* the study aims to estimate. Its necessary attributes vary by purpose and typically include **target population**, **outcome and its measurement**, **exposure or intervention conditions and comparison when relevant**, **time horizon**, and **population summary or contrast**. In interventional studies, handling post-assignment events (such as discontinuation) may also matter.

### 2. Three technical terms
| Term | Precise meaning | Illustration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Estimand** | Population-level target parameter | Difference in 12-month fall risks between slow and faster walking groups in a defined population |
| **Estimator** | Statistical rule applied to sample observations | Difference of the two sample risk proportions |
| **Estimate** | Observed numerical output of the estimator | 0.12 = 12 percentage points |

**Hypothetical teaching data with full 12-month ascertainment:**

| Baseline gait speed | Number of participants | Participants with ≥1 fall in 12 months | Estimated risk |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Slow, <0.8 m/s | 100 | 30 | 30% |
| Faster, ≥0.8 m/s | 100 | 18 | 18% |

Thus **estimated RD = 0.30 − 0.18 = 0.12 (12 percentage points)**; **estimated RR = 0.30/0.18 ≈ 1.67**. These are **unadjusted, descriptive contrasts between observed groups**, and do not establish effects of changing gait speed. The population estimand remains unknown; observed estimates are subject to uncertainty and bias. The cut point is illustrative and needs justification.

### 3. How the aim changes the target and the evidence
| Aim | Example target quantity | Data/evidence implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | Proportion of adults ≥65 with ≥1 fall within 12 months | Defined target population; representative/appropriate sampling and valid ascertainment |
| Associational | 12-month fall-risk difference between defined gait-speed groups | Baseline speed and later falls for both groups; confounding/selection consideration |
| Causal | Difference in fall risk under assignment to a specified **balance-training program** vs usual care | Clear intervention strategies and causal design/identification assumptions |
| Predictive | Individual conditional 12-month fall probability given age, gait speed, fall history, etc. | Pre-outcome predictor measurements, longitudinal outcomes, out-of-sample validation |

**Key causal subtlety:** The observational slow-versus-fast gait comparison is **not the same target** as the effect of assigning a balance-training program. Defining an actionable intervention changes the question. If \(Y^{1}\) denotes a binary fall outcome under assignment to the program and \(Y^{0}\) under usual-care assignment, a causal risk-difference target is \(E[Y^{1}]-E[Y^{0}]\). Assignment effects and effects of adhering to treatment may differ when participants do not follow the assigned program. These potential outcomes are conceptual; identifying their contrast from observed data requires assumptions.

### 4. The dataset must contain the needed evidence
Suppose a dataset holds only `participant_id, age, sex, gait_speed, previous_falls` measured at a single visit. It **cannot** identify whether a participant fell *during the subsequent 12 months*. It can support a different question about gait speed and past fall history, with a different temporal interpretation and estimand.

Future fall risk requires follow-up; losses to follow-up, death as a competing event, and variation in observed time may affect whether 12-month risks are identified. Incidence rates require relevant event counting and person-time (rather than only a simple yes/no 12-month indicator).

### 5. How the research target guides design
1. **Research question:** What scientific knowledge do we seek?
2. **Estimand:** Exactly which population quantity or contrast would answer it?
3. **Evidence requirements:** Which participants, variables, assessment times, and observation mechanisms are needed?
4. **Study design and analysis:** Which designs make those data and assumptions plausible, and which estimators match the target?

- A **prospective cohort** can measure baseline gait speed and subsequent falls for an associational question.
- A **randomized trial** could examine assignment to balance training vs usual care for a specified causal strategy; observational causal studies remain possible with defensible assumptions.
- A **prediction study** needs predictor measurements at the prediction time, subsequent outcomes, and suitable internal/external evaluation.

Choice of statistical method follows identification of the target; a significant coefficient without a clear estimand does not retroactively establish a research objective.

### 6. Practical pre-analysis protocol sentence
*In [target population], we aim to estimate [specified population quantity] comparing or describing [defined conditions], using [defined outcome] over [specified period], based on evidence collected through [study design].*

This is a planning check, not a universal sentence template. The choice between risk and rate, crude and adjusted associations, and assignment vs adherence effects alters the question/target and may alter the necessary evidence.
